**Tier-on-tier shutters** split each window into separately hinged upper and lower panels. That classic layout suits many Georgian, Victorian, Edwardian and listed homes: the joinery looks traditional, and you can open the top for a garden view while the bottom stays closed at sofa height.

We recently fitted a white tier-on-tier set to a tall period window in Leicestershire — sage walls, white architrave and a radiator under the sill.

## How this layout works

Unlike full-height shutters (one leaf from sill to head), tier-on-tier lets you:

- Fold the **top** fully back for trees and daylight
- Keep the **bottom** closed for privacy, with louvres open or shut
- Close both tiers and still tweak slat angle for a softer room

If you are still choosing a layout, our guide to [full-height vs tier-on-tier](/choosing-the-perfect-style-full-height-vs-tier-on-tier-shutters/) walks through the trade-offs.

## The install

### Top open, bottom closed

Upper panels folded back against the reveal; lower panels closed across the glass. This is the layout most period living rooms actually use during the day.

![White tier-on-tier shutters with top panels open and bottom panels closed on a sage-green wall](/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/tier-on-tier-leicestershire-open-top.jpg)

### Both tiers closed

With both tiers shut, the window still reads as fitted joinery. Louvres can sit open for daylight or closed for a more solid cover.
